02 — THE ANGLE

Why menu engineering, why now

The existing ad sells career advancement. The new creative sells a specific commercial skill that's hard to argue with: reading a menu the way the business reads it. The difference shows up in the source material — the ebook is concrete, numerical, and built around a framework chefs have never been formally taught (Stars / Plowhorses / Puzzles / Dogs).

Three hooks run through the creative system:

  • The $6,435 number — the sirloin vs scotch fillet decision, walked through as a plain-language calculation. Concrete, repeatable, instantly memorable.
  • The four-quadrant menu matrix — Stars, Plowhorses, Puzzles, Dogs. Vocabulary that gives the chef a language they didn't have before.
  • The owner conversation — the meeting where food cost is up two points and you're meant to have answers. The book is positioned as the thing that puts you on the right side of that conversation.

05 — AD COPY

Primary text variants

Five primary text options, written in the same flowing chef voice as the existing live ad. No formulaic parallel structure, no marketing fragments. Each one teases a specific piece of content from inside the book to build desire.

V1 · The eye for the menu

I spent a long time in kitchens before I really understood this. Most chefs look at a menu and see the food, which is exactly what we're trained to do. The owner is looking at the same piece of paper and seeing the single biggest financial document in the venue, and until a chef learns to read it that way too, they're only doing half the job.

The Menu Engineering Guide is the short, practical book I wrote to close that gap.

Tap below for a copy and start reading your own menu the second way this week.

V2 · The owner question

Sooner or later every owner is going to point at the menu and ask which dishes are actually making money. The chefs who can answer with a number are the ones who get pulled into the rest of the conversation about how the business is run. They get handed the menu, they get pulled into the costing meetings, they get asked first when something needs to change.

The Menu Engineering Guide gives you that number.

If you want to be that chef in that meeting, the link's right below.

V3 · The number told as a story

There's a steak example in this book that I keep coming back to. You take sirloin, which contributes about $23.50 a plate, and you compare it to scotch fillet, which when it's priced and positioned the right way contributes about $29. That's $5.50 a dish. Sell forty-five a week through one season and you've put $6,435 onto the bottom line from one decision that took thirty seconds to make.

That's the kind of thing this book teaches you to see — not a clever trick, just the practice of looking at your menu the way the business looks at it.

Tap below to grab a copy. There are dozens more decisions like that one in the book.

V4 · Seeing it differently

Once you've read this book you can't really look at a menu the same way again, and that turns out to be the whole point. You start seeing the bestseller that's punishing the grill for almost no return, the high-margin dish hiding in the wrong column, the contribution leaking quietly out of every section.

The Menu Engineering Guide is the short, practical book that gives you the eye for it.

Tap the link below and start seeing it on your own menu this week.

V5 · The office conversation

You're in the office with the owner, the P&L is open in front of you, and food cost is up two points. You can nod and promise to look into it, or you can open the menu and walk them through where the leak actually is. That second conversation is a different career, and the Menu Engineering Guide is the book that puts you into it.

If you want to walk into that next office meeting differently, the book's below.
